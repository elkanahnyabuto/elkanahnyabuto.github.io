Research

Learning Dependence from Data

My research focuses on statistical methods for estimating spatial, temporal and network dependence structures, particularly when the underlying relationships are unknown and must be learned from observations.

I develop and apply regularised estimation methods, combining spatial statistics, statistical learning and computational approaches to address problems in environmental science, financial markets and infectious-disease epidemiology.

Research overview

Estimating Unknown Dependence Structures

Many spatial and spatiotemporal statistical models require a dependence structure to be specified before estimation. These structures are commonly represented through spatial weight matrices constructed using geographic adjacency, distance, sector membership or other predefined relationships.

Although convenient, such specifications may impose assumptions that are not adequately supported by the observed data.

My research investigates how dependence matrices can instead be estimated directly from observations using regularisation and statistical learning methods. Particular attention is given to sparse estimation, model selection, numerical stability, uncertainty and predictive performance.

Methodological Research and Applications

Environmental statistics

Spatial and Temporal Modelling of Air Pollution

Estimation of spatial and temporal autoregressive effects using LASSO, with an application to hourly particulate matter concentrations in Bavaria, Germany.

The research examines regularised model selection and predictive validation for high-frequency environmental observations.

Financial statistics

Learning Volatility Dependence Networks

Development of penalised spatiotemporal ARCH models for estimating volatility dependence networks among UK equities.

Learned dependence structures are evaluated against alternative specifications based on sector membership, distance, correlation and autoregressive similarity.

Epidemiological modelling

Learning Epidemiological Connectivity

Investigation of data-driven dependence structures in endemic–epidemic models for malaria surveillance data.

The research examines how epidemiological relationships can be learned from disease observations rather than specified exclusively through geographic proximity.

Research direction

Current and Future Directions

My ongoing work seeks to strengthen the statistical foundations of data-driven dependence estimation, particularly in settings where several network specifications may explain the observations similarly well.

Areas of interest include uncertainty in learned dependence structures, stability of network estimates, model comparison and the consequences of network uncertainty for statistical prediction.
